Deep in the remote northern reaches of Death Valley National Park lies the **Racetrack Playa**, an ancient dry lakebed so completely flat that the northern end sits merely four centimeters higher than the southern end over four and a half kilometers.

Scattered across this desolate cracked mud are hundreds of heavy dolomite and syenite boulders, some weighing up to 700 pounds (320 kilograms). Trailing behind each boulder is a deep, clearly incised furrow cut into the sun-baked clay, stretching for hundreds of yards in graceful curves, zig-zags, and sharp right-angle turns.

Yet for nearly a century after prospectors first documented the phenomenon in 1915, **not a single human being had ever witnessed a rock in motion**.

## 1. The Century of Speculation: Aliens, Magnetism, and Hurricanes

Over the decades, the "sailing stones" inspired wild theories. Visitors posited subterranean magnetic anomalies, hurricane-force vortex winds, acoustic levitation, and supernatural forces. 

Early scientific attempts to solve the puzzle in the 1950s and 1970s marked the rocks with wooden stakes and surveyed their positions annually. The stakes confirmed the rocks were indeed moving—some traveling over 200 meters in a single winter—but the physical mechanism remained invisible.

Key scientific challenges baffled researchers:
* **Parallel Trails:** Rocks located hundreds of yards apart would suddenly execute identical synchronized turns, suggesting a shared, wide-area force.
* **No Animal Tracks:** The dry mud surrounding the furrows was pristine, with no footprints, tire tracks, or signs of human disturbance.
* **Immovable Masses:** Certain rocks weighed several hundred pounds, requiring forces far exceeding the strongest recorded desert wind gusts across bare dry mud.

## 2. The Norris Brothers and the GPS Experiment

In 2011, paleobiologist **Dr. Richard Norris** of the Scripps Institution of Oceanography and his cousin, engineer **James Norris**, determined to solve the mystery using modern technology.

With permits from the National Park Service, they fitted fifteen replacement rocks with custom, hermetically sealed, high-precision GPS units programmed to activate and log data whenever motion was detected. They also erected a miniature weather station on the edge of the playa.

For two years, the desert was silent. The GPS units logged zero movement.

Then came December 2013.

---

## 3. The Perfect Winter Storm: The "Ice Raft" Discovery

In late November 2013, an unusual storm dumped seven centimeters of rain across the desert basin, creating a shallow, temporary lake that flooded the southern end of the playa. That night, freezing desert temperatures dropped to minus three degrees Celsius, coating the water with a layer of clear ice just three to six millimeters thick—a sheet as thin as window glass.

On the morning of December 20, 2013, the Norris cousins returned to the playa and observed the impossible with their own eyes:
1. Midday sunlight began melting the fragile ice sheet, breaking it into vast, floating sheets tens of meters across.
2. A gentle, persistent breeze blowing at just 10 to 15 kilometers per hour (a mere light breeze) caught the surface of the floating ice floes.
3. The vast ice sheets acted as colossal natural sails, exerting immense cumulative force against the bases of the submerged boulders.
4. Slowly, almost imperceptibly, the massive rocks began to glide across the saturated, slippery mud below at two to six meters per minute, gouging fresh furrows into the lakebed.

The stones weren't moving through mysterious desert forces—they were sailing across slick liquid mud on paper-thin sheets of melting ice.

## 4. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### What causes the sailing stones in Death Valley to move?
The stones move due to a rare combination of winter rain, overnight freezing that forms thin "windowpane" ice sheets, followed by gentle morning winds that push the melting ice floes against the boulders across slick mud.

### Has anyone ever seen the rocks move in person?
Yes. Scientists Dr. Richard Norris and James Norris observed and photographed the rocks moving in real time in December 2013, with motion verified by onboard GPS sensors.

### Can visitors touch or move the sailing stones?
No. The stones and their historic trails are strictly protected by federal law in Death Valley National Park. Visitors are forbidden from moving or tampering with the rocks or walking on the playa when it is wet.
